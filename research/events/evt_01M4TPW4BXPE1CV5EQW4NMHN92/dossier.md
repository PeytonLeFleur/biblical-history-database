## Biblical references

- Daniel 5:1–29 (narration)

## Biblical account

Belshazzar feasts with a thousand lords and drinks from the Jerusalem temple vessels while praising idols. A hand writes on the plaster wall by the lampstand. The king's experts fail; the queen points to Daniel. Daniel refuses the rewards, recalls Nebuchadnezzar's humbling, condemns Belshazzar's pride and sacrilege, and reads MENE, MENE, TEKEL, UPHARSIN as the end of his kingdom. Belshazzar makes him third ruler.

## Historical setting

**Period:** Final years of the Neo-Babylonian Empire (Nabonidus, 556–539 BC)

Nabonidus spent about ten years at Tayma, entrusting "the kingship" to his eldest son (Verse Account ii 5–6); the Chronicle records "the prince" in Akkad in those years. In Nabonidus's 12th year oaths were sworn by Nabonidus "and Belshazzar, the son of the king" (Dougherty pp. 96–97). Nabonidus was back in Babylon for the New Year of year 17, when Cyrus invaded. [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]

### Rulers

- Belshazzar: Son of the king; acting ruler in Babylon ("king" in Daniel) [1, 3]
- Nabonidus: King of Babylon [5]

## Competing reconstructions

- **Historical feast on the night of the fall** (D. Guzik, R. P. Dougherty (1929)): The feast is the festival Herodotus and Xenophon place on the night Babylon was taken; the "third ruler" reflects Belshazzar's position as second to Nabonidus. [8, 3, 9, 10]
- **Court legend with historical memories** (J. J. Collins (1984), C.-L. Seow (2003), via Wikipedia): A tale of court contest written long after the events: it correctly remembers Belshazzar but makes him king and son of Nebuchadnezzar, and introduces an unknown Darius the Mede. [11, 12]

## Claims and evidence

### Belshazzar was a real person, the eldest son of Nabonidus.

**Classification:** Independently corroborated (machine proposed) · layer: evidence

Nabonidus's own Ur cylinder names Bel-šar-uṣur as his eldest son, and contemporary oath formulas name "Belshazzar, the son of the king".

- *Supporting (direct):* Nabonidus prays to Sin for Bel-šar-uṣur, "the eldest son — my offspring". (Closing prayer) [2]
- *Supporting (direct):* Contract tablets of Nabonidus's 12th year record oaths by the decrees of Nabonidus, king of Babylon, "and Belshazzar, the son of the king" — the only known oaths naming a king's son. (pp. 96–97) [3]

### Belshazzar exercised royal authority in Babylonia while Nabonidus was away at Tayma.

**Classification:** Independently corroborated (machine proposed) · layer: evidence

The Verse Account says Nabonidus "entrusted the kingship" to his eldest son; the Chronicle has "the prince" with the army in Akkad in the Tayma years; oaths in year 12 pair his name with the king's.

- *Supporting (direct):* Nabonidus gave the army to his eldest son and "entrusted the kingship to him" before going to Tayma. (col. ii 5–6) [1]
- *Supporting (direct):* Contract tablets of Nabonidus's 12th year record oaths by the decrees of Nabonidus, king of Babylon, "and Belshazzar, the son of the king" — the only known oaths naming a king's son. (pp. 96–97) [3]
- *Supporting (indirect):* The king was in Tayma while "the prince, his officers and army" were in Akkad. (ii 5 (year 7) and years 9–11) [4]

*Limitations:* The Verse Account is hostile Persian-period polemic; the Chronicle does not name the prince.

### Belshazzar was "king" of Babylon and the son of Nebuchadnezzar (Dan 5:1–2, 11, 18, 22).

**Classification:** Disputed (requires review) · layer: reconstruction

Babylonian documents call him "son of the king", never king, and do not date by his years; he was Nabonidus's son. Conservative interpreters read "king" as functional co-regency (kingship entrusted to him) and "father" as ancestor or royal predecessor, possibly through a mother descended from Nebuchadnezzar (a proposal, e.g. Shea 1982, via Wikipedia). Critical scholars read both as historical errors of a later author.

- *Supporting (direct):* Nabonidus gave the army to his eldest son and "entrusted the kingship to him" before going to Tayma. (col. ii 5–6) [1]
- *Challenging (indirect):* Contemporary texts call him mār šarri ("son of the king"); no document is dated by his regnal years and he never bore the title šarru. (Title and status) [12]
- *Challenging (indirect):* Belshazzar was Nabonidus's son, not Nebuchadnezzar's, and never became king; Darius the Mede is unknown to history. (Historical problems (Seow 2003, pp. 4–6)) [11]
- *Context (indirect):* Josephus equates Daniel's Baltasar with Naboandelus (Nabonidus), showing an ancient attempt to reconcile Daniel with Babylonian king-lists. (Ant. 10.11.2) [13]

*Limitations:* Shea 1982 not read.

### The "third ruler" in the kingdom (5:7, 16, 29) reflects rule by Nabonidus and Belshazzar.

**Classification:** Historically plausible (machine proposed) · layer: interpretation

With Nabonidus as king and Belshazzar as his deputy, the third place was the highest available; first proposed by Grotius and argued by Dougherty. Torrey and others read the Aramaic as an official title ("triumvir"-type rank) rather than a numerical rank.

- *Supporting (indirect):* Nabonidus was first ruler and Belshazzar second, so the interpreter received third place; the view goes back to Grotius. Torrey read "third" differently. (pp. 196–197 and nn. 651–654) [3]
- *Context (direct):* Contract tablets of Nabonidus's 12th year record oaths by the decrees of Nabonidus, king of Babylon, "and Belshazzar, the son of the king" — the only known oaths naming a king's son. (pp. 96–97) [3]

*Limitations:* Torrey's and Montgomery's arguments known only through Dougherty's notes.

### A royal feast was being held in Babylon on the night of its capture.

**Classification:** Historically plausible (machine proposed) · layer: evidence

Herodotus and Xenophon independently of Daniel place a festival in Babylon on the night of capture. Neither cuneiform account mentions a feast, and the Greek sources date from a century or more after the events.

- *Supporting (indirect):* Those in the middle of the city were "holding a festival" and did not know it had been taken. (1.191) [9]
- *Supporting (indirect):* Cyrus attacked on a night of "high festival" when the citizens drank all night. (7.5.15) [10]

*Limitations:* Greek accounts conflict with the Chronicle on how the city was entered.

### Temple vessels from Jerusalem were kept in Babylon until the Persian period.

**Classification:** No independent corroboration found (machine proposed) · layer: text

Known only from biblical texts (Dan 1:2; 2 Kgs 24:13; Ezra 1:7–11); no Babylonian inventory of them has been found.


### A hand miraculously wrote on the palace wall.

**Classification:** Not empirically testable (machine proposed) · layer: text

A claim of divine action that historical methods cannot assess. This is not a theological verdict.

## Commentary (interpretation, not historical evidence)

- Guzik reads Belshazzar as co-regent with Nabonidus (from 553 BC), explains "father" as grandfather or royal predecessor, and the "third ruler" as the highest rank Belshazzar could give.
