## Biblical references

- Daniel 9:1–2 (superscription): First year of Darius son of Ahasuerus.
- Daniel 9:3–19 (narration): The prayer.

## Biblical account

In the first year of Darius son of Ahasuerus, a Mede made king over the Chaldean realm, Daniel learns from the books that Jeremiah's word allotted seventy years to Jerusalem's desolations. He seeks God with fasting, sackcloth and ashes, confesses that Israel has sinned and that the curse written in the law of Moses has justly come upon it, and pleads for mercy on the desolate sanctuary and the city called by God's name.

## Historical setting

**Period:** Narrative setting: the first year after Babylon's fall to the Persians (539/538 BC on the text's own sequence); composition date disputed

Daniel dates the prayer by Darius the Mede, whom no external source names (see the Daniel 5–6 records and their open review). Babylon fell to Cyrus's army in October 539; Cyrus's first Babylonian year began 24 March 538. The prayer's concern — the end of seventy years and the desolate Temple — matches the period before the return. [1, 2]

- Cyrus II (the Great): King of Babylon and the lands from Oct 539 [1]

## Competing reconstructions

- **Dated by the fall of Babylon (539/538 BC)** (This project's model-independent constraint (operator inference)): Counting Darius's first year from his receiving the kingdom at Belshazzar's death gives c. Oct 539 – 537. This depends only on the text's sequence, not on who Darius was. [1]
- **Darius = Gubaru/Ugbaru, Cyaxares II, or Cyrus himself** (See the Darius the Mede record (Dan 5–6)): Each identification places the first year in 539/538; Jerome rejects identification with Darius I (Porphyry) or Darius III. [5]
- **Darius the Mede as a literary conflation (critical view)** (S. R. Driver (1900)): If the author confused the Persian Darius with a Median ruler, the "first year" is a narrative date only. [2]

## Claims and evidence

### Onias III, the legitimate high priest, was murdered near Antioch at the instigation of Menelaus (c. 171 BC).

**Classification:** Disputed (requires review) · layer: evidence

2 Maccabees narrates the murder at Daphne; Driver reports that historians generally accept it (Ewald, Schürer, Graetz). But 2 Maccabees alone records it, and Josephus elsewhere (B.J.) has Onias flee to Egypt and found the Leontopolis temple; Wellhausen rejected the 2 Maccabees account (as reported by Driver). The identification of Onias with the "anointed one" of 9:26 is a separate, interpretive question.

- *Supporting (direct):* Onias, in sanctuary at Daphne near Antioch, is lured out and killed by Andronicus at Menelaus's request; the king later executes Andronicus. (2 Macc 4:33–38) [6]
- *Supporting (indirect):* The account "is accepted generally by historians" (Ewald, Schürer, Graetz). (p. 140 n. 1) [2]
- *Challenging (indirect):* 2 Maccabees alone records it; Josephus (B.J. 1.1.1; 7.10.2–3) has Onias himself flee to Egypt and found the temple at Leontopolis; Wellhausen rejected the account. (p. 140 n. 1) [2]
- *Challenging (direct):* "Onias, the high priest, fled to Ptolemy" and built a temple in the nome of Heliopolis — a different end for the high priest named Onias. (B.J. 1.1.1) [7]

*Limitations:* Whether Josephus's Onias in B.J. 1.1.1 is Onias III or his son (as in Ant. 12.9.7) is itself disputed.

### 1 Nisan of Artaxerxes I's twentieth year fell on 14 March 445 BC.

**Classification:** Disputed (requires review) · layer: reconstruction

Anderson derives 14 March from the astronomical new moon of 13 March 445 BC computed for him at Greenwich, assuming no intercalary month. Parker & Dubberstein's Babylonian table (built from attested and reconstructed intercalations) places 1 Nisan of year 19 on 26 Mar 446 and of year 20 on 13 Apr 445 — 384 days apart, i.e. a thirteen-month year with an intercalary month (operator arithmetic from the table; whether that intercalation is attested or reconstructed was not checked in the plate). The Judean calendar of Nehemiah's day need not have intercalated as Babylon did, so the table does not prove Anderson wrong, but his date is not the Babylonian one. Separately, the 173,880-day interval: in the proleptic Julian calendar 14 Mar 445 BC to 6 Apr AD 32 is 173,882 days (Julian day numbers; operator calculation); Anderson reaches 173,880 by counting the end-days inclusively and adding only 116 leap days, applying the Gregorian three-in-four-centuries correction to Julian dates (his own footnote).

- *Supporting (direct):* New moon 13 March 445 BC at 7h 9m (Jerusalem), from Airy's letter of 26 June 1877; "the 1st Nisan may be assigned to the 14th March". (pp. 123–124 and note) [8]
- *Challenging (direct):* 1 Nisan of year 20 = 13 April 445 BC in the Babylonian calendar. (Table, Artaxerxes I year 20) [1]
- *Context (direct):* 476 years × 365 + 24 days (inclusive) + 116 leap days = 173,880; the note explains the Gregorian correction of three leap years in four centuries. (p. 128 and note) [8]

*Limitations:* Judean vs Babylonian intercalation not established for 446/445. P&D Plate I (attested vs reconstructed intercalations) not read in the OCR.

### Ancient Jewish and Christian chronographers overestimated the length of the Persian period.

**Classification:** Historically plausible (machine proposed) · layer: evidence

Driver and Montgomery (after Schürer) cite Demetrius and Josephus as overestimating the interval from the exile to the Hellenistic period by decades — the basis on which critical scholars explain the 67-year excess in a Maccabean reading. Demetrius's figures are textually uncertain (Behrmann, as reported by Driver), and the primary texts were not read here.

- *Supporting (indirect):* Josephus reckons 639 years from Cyrus's second year to AD 70 and other overestimates; Demetrius reckons 573 years from the fall of Samaria to Ptolemy IV — about 73 too many. (p. 147) [2]

*Limitations:* Demetrius and Josephus not read directly on this point.
