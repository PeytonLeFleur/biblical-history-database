## Biblical account

A year after the dream, walking on his palace roof, the king boasted that he had built great Babylon by his own power. A voice from heaven announced that the kingdom had departed from him; he was driven from men, ate grass like oxen and was wet with dew until his hair and nails grew long. At the end of the days his reason returned, he blessed the Most High, and he was restored with greater majesty.

## Historical setting

**Period:** Reign of Nebuchadnezzar II

Nebuchadnezzar was renowned as a builder at Babylon; most of his roughly fifty known inscriptions concern building, such as the East India House foundation tablet. Nabonidus, the last Neo-Babylonian king, was absent at the Arabian oasis of Tayma for years while "the prince" held Babylonia (Nabonidus Chronicle), and a Qumran text (4Q242) tells of Nabonidus's seven-year affliction at Tayma. ## Competing reconstructions

- **A historical illness of Nebuchadnezzar** (D. Guzik; conservative interpreters): Babylonian royal records would not record a king's incapacity; Guzik cites Abydenus (via Eusebius) on Nebuchadnezzar being "possessed" and a gap in records c. 582–575 BC. Some read the fragmentary text BM 34113 as relating to Nebuchadnezzar's later years (not verified here; the museum catalogues it as concerning Amel-Marduk). [7, 8]
- **A Nabonidus tradition transferred to Nebuchadnezzar** (J. J. Collins (1984), W. von Soden, Livius.org): Nabonidus's long absence at Tayma, rumours of his illness, and the Qumran Prayer of Nabonidus (seven years, Tayma, a Judean, acknowledgement of God) suggest the story originally concerned him. [9, 4, 10, 3]
- **A medical condition (boanthropy)** (D. Guzik): The symptoms resemble a recognised delusional condition in which a person believes himself an animal. This is an interpretive medical analogy, not a diagnosis supported by Babylonian evidence. [7]

## Claims and evidence

### Nebuchadnezzar carried out extensive building works at Babylon (Dan 4:30).

**Classification:** Independently corroborated (machine proposed) · layer: evidence

His own foundation inscriptions (e.g. the East India House Inscription, found at Babylon) and the record of his restoration of Esagila, Etemenanki, the Ishtar Gate and palaces attest the building programme.

- *Supporting (direct):* A foundation tablet of Nebuchadnezzar from Babylon records his temple building and care for the shrines of Babylon and Borsippa. (Description) [1]
- *Supporting (indirect):* Nebuchadnezzar restored Esagila, completed Etemenanki, rebuilt the Ishtar Gate and Processional Street, and built the North and Summer palaces; most of his inscriptions concern building. (Building works section) [2]

*Limitations:* Corroborates the setting of the boast, not the episode.

### Nebuchadnezzar was afflicted with a period of madness and later restored to rule.

**Classification:** Disputed (requires review) · layer: reconstruction

No cuneiform text consulted attests such an episode. Conservative interpreters argue that royal records omit failures and point to a late Greek report (Abydenus, via Guzik; not verified) and the debated fragment BM 34113 (not accessed). Critical scholars see a transferred Nabonidus tradition. There is material disagreement.

- *Challenging (indirect):* States that cuneiform texts reveal nothing about Nebuchadnezzar's mental health and argues the story was originally about Nabonidus. (Discussion) [10]
- *Context (direct):* The king (Nabonidus) was in Tayma while the prince, officers and army were in Akkad, and the New Year festival was not held. (Years 7, 9, 10, 11) [3]
- *Context (direct):* 4Q242 has Nabonidus report an ulcer lasting seven years at Tayma, forgiven and healed by God, with a Judean urging him to proclaim it. (Content) [4]

*Limitations:* BM 34113, Grayson BHLT and Abydenus were not read.

### Nabonidus spent several years at Tayma while "the prince" remained in Babylonia.

**Classification:** Independently corroborated (machine proposed) · layer: evidence

The Nabonidus Chronicle directly records "the king was in Tayma" in years 7, 9, 10 and 11, with the prince and army in Akkad and the Akitu festival not held.

- *Supporting (direct):* The king (Nabonidus) was in Tayma while the prince, officers and army were in Akkad, and the New Year festival was not held. (Years 7, 9, 10, 11) [3]

### A Qumran text (4Q242) narrates Nabonidus's seven-year affliction at Tayma and its healing by God.

**Classification:** Independently corroborated (machine proposed) · layer: evidence

The manuscript exists (Israel Museum) and its content is described in the scholarly literature summarised by Wikipedia; it is a Jewish literary text of the 1st century BC (manuscript date), not a Babylonian record.

## Commentary (interpretation, not historical evidence)
