# The thirty-day decree and Daniel in the lions' den (Dan 6:4–23)

## Biblical references

- Daniel 6:4–23 (narration)

## Biblical account

Finding no fault in Daniel, the officials have the king sign a thirty-day decree, unalterable "according to the law of the Medes and Persians". Daniel keeps praying three times daily toward Jerusalem; he is accused, and the king, bound by his decree, has him thrown into the lions' den, sealed with royal signets. At dawn Daniel is found unharmed: his God sent an angel to shut the lions' mouths.

## Historical setting

**Period:** Reign of Darius the Mede (undated)

The idea that a sealed royal edict could not be revoked appears also in Esther (1:19; 8:8), another biblical text set at the Persian court. No Persian source states such a rule; Diodorus's story of Darius III, often cited, says only that the king could not undo an execution already carried out. Mesopotamian kings did keep captured lions: Assyrian reliefs show lions released from cages for royal hunts, and Ashurnasirpal II describes caging lion cubs. No evidence of a Persian-period lion pit at Babylon has been found in the sources consulted. [1, 2, 3]

## Competing reconstructions

- **Historical episode at the Persian court** (D. Guzik, traditional interpreters): Signed Persian decrees bound even the king; the plot exploited this to remove Daniel. [4]
- **Court tale paralleling Daniel 3** (C.-L. Seow (2003), J. J. Collins (1984), via Wikipedia): A folktale of the Persian or Hellenistic period with the same pattern as the furnace story: jealous rivals, an edict, deliverance and royal confession. [5]

## Claims and evidence

### A signed decree of the Medes and Persians could not be altered, even by the king.

**Classification:** No independent corroboration found (machine proposed) · layer: reconstruction

Attested only in Daniel and Esther (1:19; 8:8), both biblical. Diodorus 17.30, commonly cited, describes a king unable to undo an execution, not a legal rule. Behistun speaks of lands obeying the king's decrees, which says nothing about irrevocability.

- *Context (indirect):* Darius III regretted executing Charidemus, but "all his royal power was not able to undo what was done" — irreversibility of a death, with no law mentioned. (17.30.6) [1]
- *Context (indirect):* Darius I says the lands "have conformed to my decrees" — royal law as binding on subjects, not on the king. (DB i 18–26 (§8)) [2]

*Limitations:* Modern Achaemenid legal studies not consulted.

### Mesopotamian kings kept captive lions.

**Classification:** Historically plausible (machine proposed) · layer: evidence

Assyrian reliefs (as summarised by Wikipedia, citing Reade 1998 and Oates & Oates 2001) show caged lions released for royal hunts, and Ashurnasirpal II is reported to have caged lion cubs at Kalhu. The reliefs and the inscription were not examined directly here, and the evidence is Assyrian, not Persian.

- *Supporting (indirect):* The den reflects "the custom which existed already among the Assyrians" and was passed to the Persians, "of keeping lions for the chase" (Bevan); cf. Ezek 19:9. (pp. 73–74 (on 6:7)) [6]
- *Supporting (indirect):* Refers to the royal zoological gardens of Assyria (Meissner) and to the capture of lions (Ezek 19). (on ch. 6 (Note)) [7]
- *Context (indirect):* Captured lions released from cages for Ashurbanipal's hunts; Ashurnasirpal II caged lion cubs at Kalhu. (Description (Reade 1998; Oates & Oates 2001)) [3]

*Limitations:* Secondary summary only; British Museum records and the Ashurnasirpal text not read. Assyrian evidence (9th–7th centuries BC); nothing found for a Persian-period lion pit at Babylon.

### A royal decree forbade petitions to any god or man except the king for thirty days.

**Classification:** No independent corroboration found (machine proposed) · layer: text

No such decree is known outside Daniel.

- *Context (indirect):* Scholars treat the tale as a folktale of the Persian or Hellenistic period, closely paralleling Daniel 3. (Composition and genre) [5]

### Daniel was delivered from the lions by an angel sent by God.

**Classification:** Not empirically testable (machine proposed) · layer: text

A claim of divine action that historical methods cannot assess. This is not a theological verdict.

## Commentary (interpretation, not historical evidence)

- Guzik reads the decree as flattering the king's pride while promoting unity, notes that the claim "all" officials agreed was false since Daniel was not consulted, and treats the den, the sealed stone and the dawn rescue as foreshadowing Christ's burial and resurrection. [4]
