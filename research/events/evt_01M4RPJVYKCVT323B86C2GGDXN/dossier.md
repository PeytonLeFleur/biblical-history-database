# The earthquake in the days of Uzziah

> **Status:** Researched — not human reviewed · research status `needs_review` · revision 1 · last reviewed: not reviewed
> Programmatic validation is not historical verification. Classifications are machine-proposed unless marked as owner-reviewed.

## Biblical references

- Amos 1:1 (superscription): Dating reference for Amos's words.
- Zechariah 14:5 (reference): Recalled as a past flight, used as a comparison for a future event.

## Biblical account

Amos 1:1 dates the words Amos "saw" concerning Israel to the days of Uzziah king of Judah and Jeroboam son of Joash king of Israel, "two years before the earthquake." Zechariah 14:5 refers back to people fleeing "from before the earthquake in the days of Uzziah king of Judah" as a comparison for a future flight. Neither text describes the earthquake itself.

## Historical setting

**Period:** Iron Age IIB, first half to middle of the 8th century BC (model-dependent)

The two texts tie the earthquake to the reign of Uzziah (Azariah) of Judah, and Amos 1:1 also to Jeroboam II of Israel. Major chronological reconstructions place these reigns in overlapping periods of the first half of the 8th century BC: Thiele (1983) dates Uzziah 792/791–740/739 BC including a coregency and Jeroboam II 793–753 BC including a coregency; Albright (1945) dates them 783–742 and 786–746 BC. These dates are reported here from secondary summaries of those works. [1, 2]

## Competing reconstructions

- **Single great earthquake in 750 BC** (S. A. Austin, G. W. Franz, E. G. Frost (2000)): Debris at six sites (Hazor, Deir Alla, Gezer, Lachish, Tell Judeideh, En Haseva) is confined to the mid-8th century with ~30-year errors; a single regional event in 750 BC with an epicentre probably in Lebanon and magnitude ≥7.8, likely 8.2. Williams reports that the 750 BC date relies on Thiele's chronology and Josephus's synchronisms. [7, 8]
- **Two earthquakes a few decades apart in the mid-8th century** (E. Kagan et al. (2011), as reported by J. Williams): Williams reports that Kagan et al. found two mid-8th-century BC seismites at three Dead Sea sites, separated by up to several decades, and that Deir Alla shows two earthquake phases. The Kagan abstract itself does not state this, so this reconstruction is unconfirmed at source here. [8, 9]
- **Earthquake c. 760 BCE** (J. R. Chadwick and A. M. Maeir (2018), A. Ben-Menahem (1991, via Williams)): The Gath excavators place a regional earthquake around 760 BCE. Williams reports that Ben-Menahem proposed 759 BCE, assuming the incense incident fell on the eve of Sukkot. [10, 8]
- **Magnitude and extent overstated; some damage has other causes** (N. Ambraseys (2009, via Williams), A. Ben-Tor (via Williams, on Hazor)): Williams reports that Ambraseys judged the textual and archaeological evidence for the magnitude overstated. He also reports that Ben-Tor did not identify Yadin's earthquake indications at Hazor, and that alternatives such as soil creep at Gezer and construction-related site effects have been proposed for several sites. [8, 11]
- **Josephus: the earthquake struck during Uzziah's incense offence** (Flavius Josephus (c. AD 93–94)): Josephus ties the earthquake to Uzziah's attempt to burn incense, with a temple fissure and a landslide at "Eroge". The biblical account of that incident (2 Chr 26:16–21) does not mention an earthquake. Under Thiele's dates Uzziah's leprosy (751/750) falls near the end of the computed window, while other reconstructions place the earthquake earlier. [12, 1]

## Claims and evidence

### A strong earthquake (or earthquakes) damaged sites in the southern Levant in the middle of the 8th century BC.

**Classification:** Strongly supported (machine proposed) · layer: reconstruction

Several independent lines point to seismic damage in this period: the excavators' report at Gath, a multi-site archaeological synthesis, a reported Jerusalem destruction layer, and Dead Sea paleoseismic records as reported by a specialist catalog. Material uncertainty remains about dating precision (~±30 years), the attribution of individual sites, and whether there was one event or two.

- *Supporting (direct):* The Gath excavators state that a cataclysmic earthquake shook the region around 760 BCE, affecting the long-abandoned city atop Tell es-Safi. (Opening paragraph, p. 48) [10]
- *Supporting (indirect):* Austin et al. report earthquake-damaged masonry at six excavated sites stratigraphically confined to the mid-8th century BC (±~30 years), and infer a single regional event in 750 BC of magnitude ≥7.8, likely 8.2. (Abstract) [7]
- *Supporting (indirect):* An IAA excavation in the City of David reported an 8th-century BCE layer of vessels smashed by collapsing walls, with no signs of fire, and attributed it to an earthquake. (Announcement text) [13]
- *Supporting (indirect):* Williams reports that Kagan et al. (2011) observed two mid-8th-century BC seismites at three Dead Sea sites (Ein Feshkha, Ein Gedi, Ze'elim), separated by up to several decades. This supports seismic activity in the period but challenges the single-event reconstruction. (Summary section) [8]
- *Context (indirect):* Williams reports critiques: Ambraseys (2009) judged the evidence for the magnitude overstated; Ben-Tor did not identify Yadin's earthquake indications at Hazor; Gezer wall displacement may be soil creep; several sites show possible construction-related site effects. (Archaeoseismic site list) [8]

*Limitations:* Several lines are known only from abstracts or a secondary catalog. Site attributions to earthquake (vs. soil creep, construction defects, or human agency) are disputed at some sites. Archaeological dating windows are decades wide.

### The earthquake referred to in Amos 1:1 and Zechariah 14:5 is a mid-8th-century BC earthquake attested by archaeology and paleoseismology.

**Classification:** Historically plausible (machine proposed) · layer: reconstruction

The texts' setting under Uzziah and Jeroboam II computes to roughly the 780s–740s BC under both modelled chronologies, which overlaps the mid-8th-century seismic evidence. No external record names the earthquake or ties a specific destruction layer to the biblical reference. If there were two events, which one the texts mean is unresolved.

- *Context (contextual):* Thiele and Albright both date Uzziah's reign within the first half to middle of the 8th century BC (Thiele 792/791–740/739 incl. coregency; Albright 783–742). (Reign and chronology sections) [1]

### There was a single regional earthquake of magnitude ~8 (≥7.8) with an epicentre in Lebanon in 750 BC.

### The earthquake damaged Jerusalem.

**Classification:** Historically plausible (machine proposed) · layer: reconstruction

An IAA excavation announced an 8th-century collapse layer without burning in the City of David and attributed it to an earthquake. No peer-reviewed publication of this attribution was identified. Josephus's account of temple and landslide damage is ~850 years later and not independent of the biblical tradition.

- *Supporting (indirect):* An IAA excavation in the City of David reported an 8th-century BCE layer of vessels smashed by collapsing walls, with no signs of fire, and attributed it to an earthquake. (Announcement text) [13]
- *Context (contextual):* Josephus narrates that a great earthquake split the temple during Uzziah's incense attempt, and that a landslide at Eroge before the city blocked the roads and damaged the king's gardens. (Ant. 9.10.4 (Whiston)) [12]

*Limitations:* The attribution to an earthquake rests on the absence of fire. The dating within the 8th century is not specified.

### The earthquake struck at the moment of Uzziah's unlawful incense offering in the temple (Josephus).

**Classification:** No independent corroboration found (machine proposed) · layer: reconstruction

The only source for this link is Josephus (c. AD 93–94). The biblical account of the incense incident does not mention an earthquake, and no independent source linking the two was found. This is not a disproof.

- *Context (contextual):* Josephus narrates that a great earthquake split the temple during Uzziah's incense attempt, and that a landslide at Eroge before the city blocked the roads and damaged the king's gardens. (Ant. 9.10.4 (Whiston)) [12]

*Limitations:* Rabbinic sources were not searched in this session.

- Amos 1:1 → **direct reference** → this event (high): The superscription uses "the earthquake" as a dating reference.

## Commentary (interpretation, not historical evidence)

- Guzik presents Amos as a working sheep breeder from Tekoa called directly by God and places his ministry roughly 760–750 B.C. under Uzziah and Jeroboam II. He treats "two years before the earthquake" as of little help for dating, citing James Boice's remark that no independent record of the earthquake was known. [14]

- No peer-reviewed publication of the City of David earthquake layer was identified.
- No Assyrian or Babylonian textual record of the earthquake was found in this session.
- Do the two mid-8th-century Dead Sea seismites reported by Williams appear in Kagan et al. 2011 itself, with what ages, and does the published correction affect them?
- Which peer-reviewed or excavation publication reports the City of David destruction layer (Uziel and Chalaf)?
- Is the Amos 1:1 earthquake the earlier or the later of two events, if there were two?
