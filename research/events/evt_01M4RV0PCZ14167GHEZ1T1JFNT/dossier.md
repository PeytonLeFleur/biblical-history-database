## Biblical references

- Daniel 3:1–7 (narration)

## Biblical account

Nebuchadnezzar made a gold image sixty cubits high and six cubits wide and set it up on the plain of Dura in the province of Babylon. He summoned satraps, prefects, governors and other provincial officials to its dedication, and a herald commanded all peoples, nations and languages to fall down and worship it when the instruments sounded; whoever refused would be thrown into a burning furnace.

## Historical setting

**Period:** Reign of Nebuchadnezzar II (date not given in the Aramaic text)

The Babylonian Chronicle records a rebellion in Akkad in Nebuchadnezzar's tenth year (Kislimu–Tebetu, c. Nov 595 – Jan 594), which he crushed, killing many of his own soldiers and capturing the leader, before receiving the submission and tribute of the kings and officials of Hatti. Herodotus mentions a golden image of Bel at Babylon. [1, 2]

## Competing reconstructions

- **A loyalty assembly after the 595/594 revolt** (Proposed in conservative scholarship (e.g. W. H. Shea, AUSS 1982 — not read)): The convocation of officials is read against the chronicle's record of an internal revolt and renewed submissions. Shea's article treats extra-biblical texts in relation to the convocation; its specific argument was not accessed and is not characterised here. [4, 1]
- **Folkloric court legend** (J. J. Collins (1984), A.-J. Levine (2010), via Wikipedia): Hyperbole (the image's size, every kind of music), the folklore plot of a minister's disgrace and rehabilitation, and Daniel's absence suggest an originally independent legendary tale; the 60×6 dimensions may contrast with the 60×60 post-exilic temple (Wesselius 2002). [2]
- **Septuagint date: Nebuchadnezzar's 18th year** (Greek Daniel as rendered by Brenton): Brenton's Septuagint dates the image to Nebuchadnezzar's eighteenth year (587/586 BC on Babylonian accession reckoning, the year of Jerusalem's fall in Jer 52:29). The date is absent from the Aramaic text and from the WEB Greek Daniel; it is modelled only under a separate Septuagint chronology model. [3, 5]

## Claims and evidence

### Nebuchadnezzar faced an internal revolt in 595/594 BC and then received renewed submission from western vassals.

**Classification:** Independently corroborated (machine proposed) · layer: evidence

ABC 5 (year 10) directly records the rebellion in Akkad, its suppression, and the tribute of the kings and officials of Hatti.

- *Supporting (direct):* In Kislimu–Tebetu of year 10 there was a rebellion in Akkad; Nebuchadnezzar killed many of his own army and captured the rebel leader, then marched to Hatti where kings and officials submitted and paid tribute. (Reverse 21′–24′ (year 10)) [1]

*Limitations:* Connection to Daniel 3 is not stated in the chronicle; any link is interpretive.

### Nebuchadnezzar set up a gold image of 60 by 6 cubits on the plain of Dura and required officials to worship it.

**Classification:** No independent corroboration found (machine proposed) · layer: text

No Babylonian source mentions such an image or ceremony. Nebuchadnezzar's known inscriptions concern building and the cults of Babylonian gods. This is not a disproof.

- *Context (direct):* In Kislimu–Tebetu of year 10 there was a rebellion in Akkad; Nebuchadnezzar killed many of his own army and captured the rebel leader, then marched to Hatti where kings and officials submitted and paid tribute. (Reverse 21′–24′ (year 10)) [1]
- *Context (contextual):* Herodotus mentions a golden image of Bel at Babylon; the story's hyperbole and folklore plot are noted by Collins (1984). (Analysis) [2]

### The plain of Dura can be identified with a specific site near Babylon.

**Classification:** Disputed (requires review) · layer: reconstruction

Oppert (19th c.) proposed the Duair mound south-east of Babylon, with a reported statue pedestal; a Tigris site near Tekrit was also suggested; modern scholarship notes "Dura" may simply mean a plain or fortress. No modern excavation report confirming any identification was found.

- *Supporting (indirect):* Oppert located the plain south-east of Babylon near the mound of Dowair/Duair, where a pedestal of a huge statue was found. (Entry "Dura") [6]
- *Challenging (indirect):* "Dura" may mean "plain" or "fortress" rather than naming a specific place. (Plot/analysis) [2]

*Limitations:* Only 19th-century reports.

### Some instrument names in Daniel 3:5 are Greek loanwords.

**Classification:** Independently corroborated (machine proposed) · layer: evidence

BDB derives sumpōnyāh from late Greek symphōnia; this linguistic fact is used on both sides of the debate over the book's date (see the composition record).

- *Supporting (direct):* sumpōnyāh (Dan 3:5, 10, 15) is a loanword from late Greek symphōnia. (BDB entry) [7]

*Limitations:* Lexicon-level evidence; Mitchell & Joyce (1965) not read.

## Commentary (interpretation, not historical evidence)

- Guzik ties the all-gold image to the dream of chapter 2 as a defiant claim that Nebuchadnezzar's kingdom would not pass. He thinks the image was a gold-overlaid pillar and mentions a mound with a pedestal as a possible site. He answers critics who date the book late from the Greek instrument names by pointing to earlier Greek contacts. [8]
